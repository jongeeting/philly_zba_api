# Multifamily Housing Variance Analysis

**How Philadelphia's Zoning Code Blocks "Missing Middle" Housing**

---

## Executive Summary

Philadelphia's zoning code forces **even multifamily-designated zones** to seek variances for routine projects. RM-1, the city's primary multifamily zone, generates **160 variance appeals per year**—demonstrating that the problem isn't just single-family zoning, but overly restrictive rules across the board.

**Key Finding:** Allowing fourplexes by-right would resolve 79% of dwelling-unit-specific variance appeals, unlocking "naturally affordable" missing middle housing without subsidies.

**Duplex-only policies resolve only 5.8%—essentially useless for addressing the variance crisis.**

## The RM-1 Problem: Even Multifamily Zones Require Variances

**RM-1 (Residential Multi-family)** is Philadelphia's primary multifamily zoning designation—explicitly designed for apartment buildings. Yet it generates:

- **2,074 variance appeals** over 13 years
- **160 appeals per year**
- **12.4% of ALL citywide variance appeals**

### What's Wrong with RM-1?

Even in zones DESIGNED for multifamily, projects need variances for:
- Roof decks (amenity space)
- Parking (minimums too high for transit areas)
- Height (limits often too restrictive)
- Setbacks (inflexible rules)

**This proves the problem isn't just "single-family zoning" but overly restrictive rules everywhere.**

### RM-1 Trend

RM-1 variance appeals **dropped sharply after 2017**:
- 2013-2017 average: 119/year
- 2018-2025 average: 71/year
- **Change: -40%**

This may indicate:
- A zoning code change that helped
- Data quality issues
- Reduced development pressure

**Recommendation:** Investigate what changed in 2017-2018 for RM-1 and replicate it in other zones.

## RSA-5: The Rowhouse Conversion Problem

**RSA-5** (Residential Single-Family Attached) is Philadelphia's most common residential zoning—the rowhouse district where most Philadelphians live. It generates:

- **6,892 variance appeals** (41% of citywide total)
- **530 appeals per year**

### Why RSA-5 Matters for Multifamily

While technically "single-family," RSA-5 properties are:
- Often subdivided into apartments
- Prime candidates for 2-4 unit conversions
- Located in walkable, transit-rich neighborhoods

**Current rules force these organic conversions through the variance process**, adding:
- $5,000-15,000 per variance
- 3-6 months delay
- Neighbor opposition opportunities
- Uncertainty that kills projects

**Allowing fourplexes in RSA-5 would:**
- Legalize existing informal conversions
- Enable "missing middle" production at scale
- Preserve neighborhood character (4 units in a rowhouse is invisible from street)
- Eliminate ~30 variance appeals/year from density alone
- Enable ~240 total variance reductions (combined with parking, height, roof decks)

## Why Multifamily Matters: The "Naturally Affordable" Housing Argument

### The Problem with Subsidized Affordable Housing

- Requires government funding
- Takes years to develop
- Limited to specific sites
- Serves narrow income bands
- Costs $200,000-400,000 per unit to build

### The Promise of "Missing Middle" Housing

**Small multifamily (2-6 units) is "naturally affordable":**
- No subsidies required
- Built by small-scale developers
- Distributed throughout neighborhoods
- Serves range of incomes
- Maintains neighborhood character
- Leverages existing building stock (conversions)

**Philadelphia's rowhouse stock is PERFECT for 2-4 unit conversions:**
- Traditional building type (not "new construction")
- Already has plumbing stacks, party walls
- In walkable, transit-rich neighborhoods
- Can be invisible from street (internal conversion)
- Preserves exterior character

**Current rules make this illegal without a variance.**

## The Cost of the Variance Process

**Per project variance costs:**
- Application fees: $1,500-3,000
- Architect/lawyer fees: $3,000-10,000
- Delay costs: 3-6 months (financing, timing)
- Neighbor opposition: meetings, concessions
- **Total: $5,000-15,000 + 4 months**

**For small-scale developers:**
- A 2-4 unit conversion is a $300,000-600,000 project
- $10,000 variance cost = 2-3% of project budget
- 4-month delay = carrying costs, lost time value
- Uncertainty risk kills marginal projects

**Result:** Only large, well-capitalized developers can absorb variance costs and delays. Small-scale, incremental development becomes economically marginal.

**This is why fourplex-by-right matters:** It unlocks small-scale development that produces naturally affordable housing without subsidies.

## Recommendations

### Priority 1: Allow Fourplexes By-Right in All Residential Zones

**Where:** RSA-5, RSA-3, RM-1, RSD zones

**Impact:**
- Eliminates 29 density-related variance appeals/year
- Enables conversions of existing rowhouses
- Produces 100s of units/year of missing middle housing
- No change to exterior appearance (internal conversions)

**Aligns with:** State missing middle housing bills

### Priority 2: Fix RM-1 Rules (Even Multifamily Zones Are Too Restrictive)

**Changes needed:**
1. Eliminate parking minimums (160 appeals/year in RM-1 alone)
2. Allow 4-5 stories (current limits often too restrictive)
3. Allow roof decks by-right (key amenity)
4. Relax setback requirements

**Impact:** Reduce RM-1 variances from 160/year to ~60-80/year (60% reduction)

### Priority 3: Comprehensive Package (Tier 2)

**Reforms:** Fourplex + No parking + 4 stories + Roof decks + Commercial

**Impact:**
- 661 appeals/year eliminated (50.7% of total)
- By-right rate: 82.8% → 89.9%
- **Unlocks multifamily at scale across the city**

## Comparison to Other Cities

### Minneapolis

- Allowed triplexes citywide in 2018
- Result: Modest uptake, but removed barrier
- Philadelphia has better building stock for conversions

### California (SB 9)

- Required duplexes statewide in 2021
- Mixed results due to restrictive implementation
- Lesson: Need fourplexes, not just duplexes, for meaningful impact

### Oregon (HB 2001)

- Required fourplexes in cities >10,000
- Too early for results
- Lesson: Statewide mandate > local opt-in

### Philadelphia's Advantage

We have:
- Existing rowhouse stock PERFECT for conversions
- Strong small-scale developer community
- Walkable, transit-rich neighborhoods
- Historic precedent (rowhouses were always subdivided)

**We just need to legalize what the market wants to build.**
