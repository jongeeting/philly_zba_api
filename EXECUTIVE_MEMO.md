# Philadelphia Zoning Board of Adjustment: Variance Crisis and Reform Opportunities

**Date:** January 26, 2026
**Subject:** Analysis of ZBA Variance Patterns and Impact of Proposed Zoning Reforms (2007-2025)

---

## Executive Summary

Philadelphia's Zoning Board of Adjustment (ZBA) processes over 1,300 variance appeals per year, creating significant delays and costs for housing development. Our analysis of 18 years of ZBA data (2007-2025) reveals that **targeted zoning reforms could eliminate over 50% of variance cases** while maintaining neighborhood character. The 2012 zoning code reform improved by-right development from 80.5% to 83.2% (+2.7 points), but left substantial room for further progress.

**Key Finding:** A combination of four reforms—allowing 4-story buildings by-right, permitting fourplexes, eliminating parking minimums, and allowing roof decks—would eliminate **661 variance appeals per year** and improve Philadelphia's by-right development rate to **91.7%** (+8.5 points).

---

## Current State: The Variance Bottleneck

### The Numbers
- **1,303 variance appeals per year** (2019-2025 average)
- **83.2% by-right development rate** (17% of projects require variances)
- **Cost to developers:** ~$5,000-15,000 per variance + 3-6 month delays
- **Cost to city:** ZBA processing, staff time, neighborhood meeting requirements

### The 2012 Reform: Modest Gains
Philadelphia's 2012 zoning code reform aimed to reduce variances by updating outdated rules. Our analysis validates the city's finding of improvement, though the magnitude differs:

- **Our calculation:** 80.5% → 83.2% by-right rate (+2.7 points)
- **City's report claim:** ~68% → 72-74% (+4-6 points, with internal contradictions)

The discrepancy likely stems from how withdrawn applications are categorized. Our methodology matches the city's own published data table more closely than their narrative claims.

**Bottom line:** The 2012 reform helped, but 1,300+ annual variance appeals remain a significant obstacle to housing production.

## Root Causes: What Drives Variance Appeals?

Analysis of 16,954 variance appeals from 2013-2025 reveals the primary drivers:

| Rank | Variance Type | Appeals | Per Year | % of Appeals | % of All Projects |
|------|---------------|---------|----------|--------------|-------------------|
| 1 | Roof decks | 4,137 | 318 | 24.4% | 4.2% |
| 2 | Parking | 3,521 | 271 | 20.8% | 3.6% |
| 3 | Commercial | ~3,700 | ~285 | ~22% | ~3.8% |
| 4 | Height | 1,259 | 97 | 7.4% | 1.3% |
| 5 | Dwelling units | 921 | 71 | 5.4% | 0.9% |

*Note: Appeals can involve multiple variance types, so percentages don't sum to 100%*

### 1. **Roof Decks** (24.4% of appeals, 318/year)
The single largest variance driver. Philadelphia's restrictive accessory structure rules make roof decks require variances in most residential zones.

**Policy disconnect:** Roof decks are common urban amenities that don't meaningfully impact neighbors yet consume massive ZBA resources.

### 2. **Parking** (20.8% of appeals, 271/year)
Minimum parking rules force variances even when developers want to build less parking or when sites lack space for required spots.

**Policy disconnect:** Philadelphia has excellent transit, walkability, and street parking. Mandating off-street parking increases housing costs and reduces density.

### 3. **Commercial Uses** (~22% of appeals, ~285/year)
Neighborhood commercial uses (corner stores, cafes, offices) require variances in residential zones.

**Policy disconnect:** Traditional Philadelphia neighborhoods had corner stores; current zoning treats them as incompatible uses.

### 4. **Height** (7.4% of appeals, 97/year)
Many residential zones cap buildings at 35 feet (typically 3 stories), forcing developers seeking 4-story buildings to request variances. Most height variances cite "maximum height" violations rather than specifying story counts.

**Policy disconnect:** Four-story buildings are compatible with urban neighborhoods and common in successful Philadelphia corridors.

### 5. **Dwelling Unit Density** (5.4% of appeals, 71/year)
Single-family zoning and restrictive density limits prevent duplexes, triplexes, and small apartment buildings.

**Policy disconnect:** "Missing middle" housing types provide naturally affordable options and match Philadelphia's historic building patterns.

---

## Reform Impact Analysis: Two Tiers of Change

We analyzed two reform packages aligned with pending state "missing middle" housing legislation:

### **Tier 1: Modest Reforms** (4 Stories + Duplex + No Parking Minimums)
- **Eliminates:** 120 appeals per year (9.2%)
- **Partially helps:** 156 appeals per year (12.0%)
- **By-right rate:** 83.2% → 84.7% (+1.5 points)

**Assessment:** Meaningful but insufficient. Duplexes are too restrictive—Philadelphia needs more density to address housing shortage.

### **Tier 2: Transformative Reforms** (Add Roof Decks + Fourplex + Commercial)
- **Eliminates:** 661 appeals per year (50.7%)
- **Partially helps:** 26 appeals per year (2.0%)
- **By-right rate:** 83.2% → 91.7% (+8.5 points)

**Assessment:** Game-changing. Would reduce ZBA caseload by half while preserving variance process for genuinely exceptional projects.

### **The Roof Deck Factor**
Tier 2 eliminates **541 more appeals per year** than Tier 1—4.5x the impact. The difference is almost entirely driven by **allowing roof decks by-right**. This single reform has more impact than height, parking, and density reforms combined.

---

## Recommendations

### **Priority 1: Implement Tier 2 Reforms Immediately**
1. **Allow 4-story buildings by-right** in residential zones (35 feet → 45 feet height limits)
2. **Allow fourplexes by-right** (eliminate single-family-only zoning)
3. **Eliminate parking minimums citywide** (developers can still build parking if market demands it)
4. **Allow roof decks by-right** (remove accessory structure restrictions)
5. **Expand neighborhood commercial allowances** (corner stores, small offices in residential zones)

**Expected impact:** Eliminate 661 variance appeals per year, improve by-right rate to 91.7%

### **Priority 2: Monitor and Iterate**
The Tier 2 complete elimination rate has declined from 55.7% (2013-2018) to 43.7% (2022-2025), suggesting:
- Projects are becoming more complex
- New variance drivers may be emerging
- Additional reforms may be needed within 3-5 years

### **Priority 3: Support State Legislation**
Pennsylvania has pending "missing middle" housing bills that would require municipalities to allow duplexes and fourplexes. Our analysis provides data-driven support for these reforms, showing they would:
- Eliminate over 50% of Philadelphia's variance appeals
- Maintain neighborhood compatibility (4 stories, modest density)
- Reduce housing development costs and delays

## Methodology and Data Quality

**Data source:** Philadelphia CARTO open data API
**Variance appeals:** 16,954 appeals (2013-2025)
**Zoning permits:** 81,617 permits (2013-2025)
**Total projects:** 98,571
**Analysis period:** Post-reform (2013-2025) for impact assessment

**Validation:** Our methodology matches the city's own 5-year report data within 0.5 percentage points, providing confidence in findings.

**Limitations:** Keyword-based detection may miss some variance types. "Partially helped" estimates are conservative—some multi-issue appeals might convert to fully by-right with reforms.

---

## Conclusion

Philadelphia's zoning code creates an unnecessary variance crisis that delays housing, increases costs, and overwhelms the ZBA with routine requests. **Tier 2 reforms would cut the variance caseload in half** while allowing the ZBA to focus on genuinely exceptional cases requiring discretionary review.

The data is clear: allowing 4-story fourplexes with roof decks and no parking minimums would transform Philadelphia's development process, making 92% of projects proceed by-right. This would accelerate housing production, reduce costs, and maintain neighborhood character—all while aligning with traditional Philadelphia building patterns.

**Recommendation:** City Council should advance Tier 2 reforms immediately, starting with the highest-impact change: allowing roof decks by-right.
